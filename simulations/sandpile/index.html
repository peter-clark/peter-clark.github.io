When a cell accumulates four or more grains it topples, sending one grain to each neighbour — which may trigger further topples in a chain reaction. The Bak–Tang–Wiesenfeld model is the original example of self-organized criticality, producing scale-free avalanches and striking fractal patterns. ---

How it works

Press Start to begin dropping grains. Each site holds 0–3 grains at rest, drawn as increasing brightness — an empty site is nearly black, a site holding 3 is full orange and one grain away from toppling. A site that reaches 4 gives one grain to each of its four neighbours, which can push them over in turn. Relaxation is shown as parallel sweeps, so you watch the avalanche front travel outward a ring at a time; cells flash pale while they are mid-topple. Grains that fall off the edge are lost, and that leak is what lets the pile reach a steady state rather than filling forever.

The striking thing is that nothing here is tuned. Start from an empty lattice, a half-full one, or a maximally full one — the pile drives itself to the same critical density of about 2.125 grains per site, and stays there. Dropping at the centre builds the familiar fractal target; dropping at random sites gives cleaner avalanche statistics, since every site gets its turn as the seed.

Graphs: (a) the avalanche-size distribution N(s) on log-log axes — at criticality this is a straight line, meaning avalanches have no characteristic size, and a single grain can set off anything from nothing to a system-spanning cascade; (b) mean height against avalanche number, the self-organization itself, with the critical density marked as a dotted line. Watch it climb from an empty start or fall from a full one, then sit on the line.
