How it works

Every walker takes one unit step per tick — ±1 in one dimension, or one of four directions in two. Nothing else.

The canvas and the graphs are doing different jobs. The graphs average over the whole ensemble — thousands of walkers, none of them drawn. The canvas draws a handful of individual paths, the way sample paths are drawn in a textbook. A cloud of ten thousand dots tells you nothing the graphs do not; a dozen legible trajectories show you what a single walker actually does, which is the part worth watching. Both sliders are yours: how many walkers the statistics run over, and how many of them get drawn.

The 1-D view is the classic x(t) diagram — time runs across, position is vertical, t = 0 pinned to the left. Both axes rescale in place as the run grows, so the whole history stays on screen. The dotted envelope is ±√t, the scale the ensemble spreads at; watching individual paths wander in and out of it is the clearest picture of what "diffusive" means. The 2-D views draw each shown walker's recent path on a torus, so a walker leaving one edge returns at the opposite one and nothing is ever lost off-screen.

The four setups

1-D free and 2-D free are the plain walk. The position distribution is Gaussian and the mean squared displacement grows as ⟨r²⟩ = t, which is why the measured curve sits on the dotted reference line regardless of dimension.

1-D on [0, ∞) puts an absorbing wall at the origin. Every walker is released at x₀, and one that reaches 0 is absorbed — it stops existing, and its path simply ends there, marked with a dot on the wall. The ensemble thins out as the run goes on, so the fraction still walking is the survival probability measured directly rather than inferred.

This is the setup with exact and slightly surprising answers. The walker returns to the origin with probability 1 — every path ends eventually — and yet the mean time it takes is infinite. Both graphs show why: first-passage times follow a t−3/2 power law, a tail heavy enough that the mean diverges, and the surviving fraction decays only as t−1/2. Raise the ensemble size for a cleaner tail; the statistics come from however many walkers you started with, since none are replaced.

2-D in a field biases the x-steps by b, giving each walker a mean drift of b/2 per step. b runs either way about zero, so the paths can be driven left or right. They still spread diffusively about their centre, but that centre now moves linearly — on the torus the paths visibly stream across and wrap around. On the graph the measured ⟨r²⟩ bends up away from the diffusive line as the ballistic term takes over. Note that only the drawing wraps: positions are stored unwrapped, so ⟨r²⟩ is the true displacement rather than something that saturates at the box size.
